\begin{abstract}

Hard disk drives remain among the most frequently replaced components in storage systems, and their unexpected failure causes data loss, downtime and increased operational cost. Manufacturer-embedded SMART thresholds detect only 3--10\% of failures at acceptably low false-alarm rates. Classical methods (SVM or Naive Bayes) perform reasonably on SMART data, but most of them rely on a single model and do not combine different learning strategies. Our combination of all 4 independent models - random forest classifier, an unsupervised autoencoder anomaly detector, a UMAP clustering model and another classifier, made on the bottleneck from autoencoder. Each model captures different kind of failure signal, and each one has its own interpretational value. At the end, all 4 predictions are combined into single aggregated health index (AHI). Since each model provides its own perspective on disk health, the final AHI can be decomposed and explained through the individual contributions of each model.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Hard disk drives are among the most frequently replaced components in storage systems, and their unexpected failure is a leading cause of data loss, service unavailability, and increased operational cost~\cite{lu2020smarter}. To provide early warning, drive manufacturers embed Self-Monitoring, Analysis, and Reporting Technology (SMART) into virtually every modern drive. However, the threshold-based failure flags built into SMART firmware are deliberately conservative: manufacturers report failure detection rates of only 3--10\% at an acceptable false-alarm rate of roughly 0.1\% per year~\cite{murray2005}, leaving the majority of failures undetected until it is too late.

Among the solutions, each one has a distinct blind spot. No single model covers all failure modes: gradual degradation may go unnoticed by a supervised classifier trained on binary labels, while explicit failure signals may be missed by an unsupervised detector that never saw them. Murray et al.~\cite{murray2005} established the foundational comparison of SVMs and clustering against manufacturer thresholds but did not combine paradigms. Ahmed and Green~\cite{ahmed2024costaware} addressed class imbalance with a cost-aware LSTM but remained within a single supervised paradigm. Xu et al.~\cite{xu2018layerwise} predicted a continuous health degree via adversarial training in a semi-supervised setting, while Zhou et al.~\cite{zhou2024proactive} used reconstruction-based anomaly detection to identify deterioration modes -- both, however, committed to one learning strategy. This is where a hybrid approach that combines multiple paradigms into a single, interpretable output, is standing out.

\textbf{Contributions.} In this paper we:
\begin{itemize}
    \item combine four independently trained models -- a random forest classifier, an unsupervised autoencoder anomaly detector, a classifier built on the autoencoder bottleneck, and a UMAP clustering model -- into a single aggregated health index (AHI);
    \item propose a weighted root-mean-square aggregation, the Aggregated Health Index (AHI), that keeps the interpretability of each component while being more robust than any single model on its own;
    \item report results (best component reaches ROC-AUC 0.929 and 89.1\% failure recall) and reflect on the trade-offs of using this hybrid as a small, fully offline pipeline for data-center and edge/NAS use.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work on SMART-based failure prediction. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the dataset, preprocessing, the four component models, and the AHI aggregation formula. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports evaluation results. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses deployment use cases, trade-offs, and limitations, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Machine learning applied to raw SMART attribute values, rather than the manufacturer's own threshold flags, has been shown to improve detection rates~\cite{murray2005,lu2020smarter}. Yet the ML-based disk failure prediction remains fragmented. Supervised classifiers, such as the random forests and SVMs used by Murray et al.~\cite{murray2005}, achieve high precision when enough labeled failures are available but struggle with the extreme class imbalance typical of SMART datasets (often exceeding 10{,}000:1~\cite{ahmed2024costaware}). Unsupervised anomaly detectors, such as autoencoders trained on healthy drives only, do not require failure labels but cannot exploit known failure patterns when they exist~\cite{zhou2024proactive}. Clustering approaches group drives by operating regime and provide population-level risk context, but do not output a direct failure probability. Semi-supervised methods, such as the reconstruction-based approach of Zhou et al.~\cite{zhou2024proactive} and the health-degree prediction of Xu et al.~\cite{xu2018layerwise}, attempt to bridge this gap but still rely on a single learning strategy.

\section{Data and Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\textbf{Dataset.} We use Backblaze's 2025 hard drive SMART dataset~\cite{backblaze2025}, which covers a full calendar year of daily records. The fleet is mostly HDDs~\cite{backblaze2025stats}, and failures are rare compared to healthy records (high class imbalance).

\textbf{Two dataset variants.} We prepared two final learning datasets to match the learning setups:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Autoencoder anomaly detection:} trained on healthy-only rows sampled from many CSVs (small per-file caps to keep memory OK). A healthy hold-out split is used for early stopping and to set the anomaly threshold; failure rows are held out entirely and used only for evaluation (e.g., ROC-AUC, failure anomaly rate), not for training.
    \item \emph{Supervised classifiers and clustering:} a balanced 50:50 set by taking all failure rows we found and sampling the same number of healthy rows at random. This avoids the extreme class imbalance during training.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Preprocessing.} We convert capacity bytes to gigabytes, derive an SSD flag (\texttt{jeSSD}) from the model name, fill missing counters with 0, use simple medians for a few mechanical counters, and add three engineered features: \texttt{any\_critical\_error}, \texttt{total\_error\_count}, and \texttt{error\_per\_gb}. Neural models use MinMax scaling; the scikit-learn model uses the same processed features without the NN scaler.

\textbf{Features.} We keep a compact 19-feature vector:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{capacity\_gigabytes}, \texttt{jeSSD}
  \item SMART raw counters: 1, 3, 4, 5, 7, 9, 12, 187, 188, 191, 192, 193, 197, 198
  \item Engineered: \texttt{any\_critical\_error}, \texttt{total\_error\_count}, \texttt{error\_per\_gb}
\end{itemize}
This mixes core SMART counters with three simple engineered features.

\textbf{Models and aggregation.} We train four components on the same features: (1) a random forest classifier (scikit-learn) that outputs a failure probability; (2) a TensorFlow autoencoder trained only on healthy rows, where the reconstruction error is turned into a 0--1 anomaly score with a percentile threshold; (3) a small dense classifier trained on the autoencoder bottleneck (low-dimensional code) to predict failure probability; and (4) UMAP clustering on the bottleneck space, where cluster risk is the empirical failure rate of that cluster (outliers fall back to a default risk). To get one score, we compute an Aggregated Health Index (AHI) by a weighted root-mean-square of the four component scores and then clip the percentage for stability. We used fixed weights from validation (RF 0.30, bottleneck classifier 0.40, anomaly 0.20, clustering 0.10), and the final AHI can be explained from per-component contributions.
