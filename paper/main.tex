\begin{abstract}

Unexpected disk failure is one of the most common causes of data loss and downtime in storage systems, yet the built-in SMART warnings on modern drives catch only a small fraction of actual failures. Manufacturer-embedded SMART thresholds detect only 3--10\% of failures at acceptably low false-alarm rates. Classical methods (SVM or Naive Bayes) perform reasonably on SMART data, but most of them rely on a single model and do not combine different learning strategies. We combine 4 independently trained models -- a random forest classifier, an unsupervised autoencoder anomaly detector, a classifier built on the autoencoder bottleneck, and an HDBSCAN clustering model. Each model captures different kind of failure signal and has its own interpretational value. All 4 predictions are combined into single Aggregated Health Index (AHI). The best individual component reaches ROC-AUC 0.929 and 89.1\% failure recall, and the final AHI can be decomposed and explained through the individual contribution of each model.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{hard drive failure assessment, SMART data, hybrid model, autoencoder, anomaly detection, HDBSCAN clustering, interpretable machine learning, edge AI}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Hard disk drives are among the most frequently replaced components in storage systems, and their unexpected failure is a leading cause of data loss, service unavailability, and increased operational cost~\cite{lu2020smarter}. To provide early warning, drive manufacturers embed Self-Monitoring, Analysis, and Reporting Technology (SMART) into virtually every modern drive. However, the threshold-based failure flags built into SMART firmware are deliberately conservative: manufacturers report failure detection rates of only 3--10\% at an acceptable false-alarm rate of roughly 0.1\% per year~\cite{murray2005}, leaving the majority of failures undetected until it is too late.

Among the solutions, each one has a distinct blind spot. No single model covers all failure modes: gradual degradation may go unnoticed by a supervised classifier trained on binary labels, while explicit failure signals may be missed by an unsupervised anomaly detector that never saw them. Murray et al.~\cite{murray2005} established the foundational comparison of SVMs and clustering against manufacturer thresholds but did not combine paradigms. Ahmed and Green~\cite{ahmed2024costaware} addressed class imbalance with a cost-aware LSTM but remained within a single supervised paradigm. Xu et al.~\cite{xu2018layerwise} predicted a continuous health degree with noise-based training in a semi-supervised setting, while Zhou et al.~\cite{zhou2024proactive} used reconstruction-based anomaly detection to identify deterioration modes -- both, however, committed to one learning strategy. This is where a hybrid approach that combines multiple paradigms into a single, interpretable output stands out.

\textbf{Contributions.} In this paper we:
\begin{itemize}
    \item combine four independently trained models -- a random forest classifier, an unsupervised autoencoder anomaly detector, a classifier built on the autoencoder bottleneck, and an HDBSCAN clustering model -- into a single Aggregated Health Index (AHI) using a weighted root-mean-square formula;
    \item keep all four per-component scores visible alongside AHI, so the final output is interpretable and can be traced back to each model's contribution;
    \item report evaluation results and reflect on the trade-offs of using this hybrid as a small, fully offline pipeline for data-centre and edge/NAS deployment.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work on SMART-based failure prediction. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the dataset, preprocessing, the four component models, and the AHI aggregation formula. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports evaluation results. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses deployment use cases, trade-offs, and limitations, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Machine learning applied to raw SMART attribute values, rather than the manufacturer's own threshold flags, has been shown to improve detection rates~\cite{murray2005,lu2020smarter}. Yet ML-based disk failure prediction remains fragmented. Supervised classifiers, such as the random forests and SVMs used by Murray et al.~\cite{murray2005}, achieve high precision when enough labeled failures are available but struggle with the extreme class imbalance typical of SMART datasets (often exceeding 10{,}000:1~\cite{ahmed2024costaware}). Unsupervised anomaly detectors, such as autoencoders trained on healthy drives only, do not require failure labels but cannot exploit known failure patterns when they exist~\cite{zhou2024proactive}. Clustering approaches group drives by operating regime and provide population-level risk context, but do not output a direct failure probability. Semi-supervised methods, such as the reconstruction-based approach of Zhou et al.~\cite{zhou2024proactive} and the health-degree prediction of Xu et al.~\cite{xu2018layerwise}, attempt to bridge this gap but still rely on a single learning strategy.

\section{Data and Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\textbf{Dataset.} We use Backblaze's 2025 hard drive SMART dataset~\cite{backblaze2025}, which covers a full calendar year of daily records. The fleet is mostly HDDs~\cite{backblaze2025stats}, and failures are rare compared to healthy records (high class imbalance), which was one of the main challenges.

\textbf{Two dataset variants.} We prepared two final learning datasets to match the learning setups:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Autoencoder anomaly detection:} trained on healthy-only rows. A healthy hold-out split is used for early stopping and to set the anomaly threshold; failure rows are used only for evaluation and not for training.
    \item \emph{Supervised classifiers and clustering:} a balanced 50:50 set by taking all failure rows we found and sampling the same number of healthy rows at random. This avoids the extreme class imbalance during training. The final learning dataset is composed of 4{,}414 failure and 4{,}414 healthy records.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Preprocessing.} We convert capacity bytes to gigabytes, derive an SSD flag (\texttt{jeSSD}) from the model name, fill missing counters with 0, use medians for a few mechanical counters, and add three engineered features: \texttt{any\_critical\_error}, \texttt{total\_error\_count}, and \texttt{error\_per\_gb}. Neural models use MinMax scaling; the scikit-learn model uses the same processed features without the NN scaler.

\textbf{Features.} We use a compact set of 19 features:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{capacity\_gigabytes}, \texttt{jeSSD}
  \item SMART raw counters: 1, 3, 4, 5, 7, 9, 12, 187, 188, 191, 192, 193, 197, 198
  \item Engineered: \texttt{any\_critical\_error}, \texttt{total\_error\_count}, \texttt{error\_per\_gb}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Models and aggregation.} We train four components on the same features: (1) a random forest classifier (scikit-learn) that outputs a failure probability; (2) a TensorFlow autoencoder trained only on healthy rows, where the reconstruction error is turned into a 0--1 anomaly score with a percentile threshold; (3) a small dense classifier trained on the autoencoder bottleneck (low-dimensional code) to predict failure probability; and (4) HDBSCAN clustering on the bottleneck space, where cluster risk is the empirical failure rate of that cluster (outliers fall back to a default risk). To get one score, we compute an Aggregated Health Index (AHI) by a weighted root-mean-square of the four component scores and then clip the percentage for stability. We used fixed weights from validation (RF 0.30, bottleneck classifier 0.40, anomaly 0.20, clustering 0.10), and the final AHI can be explained from per-component contributions.

\subsection{Random Forest Classifier}

Random forest (classifier) was trained on a balanced 50:50 dataset and evaluated with 5-fold cross-validation. It reaches about 90.15\% accuracy, with a failure recall of 0.86 and precision of 0.93. The model catches most failing drives, while keeping false alarms relatively low.

\subsection{Autoencoder Anomaly Detector}

The anomaly detection model was trained on healthy rows only and evaluated against all 4{,}414 failure rows. Reconstruction error does separate failures from healthy records reasonably well, with ROC-AUC of 0.901. However, with failure recall of only 44.7\% at the 99th-percentile threshold, the model misses more than half of the actual failures. Its main value is that it does not need any failure labels during training at all, and it catches a different kind of problem than the supervised classifiers.

\subsection{Bottleneck Classifier}

The bottleneck classifier is the strongest model. It consists of two parts. First, the autoencoder compresses the 19 features into an 8-dimensional bottleneck, and then a small dense classifier is trained on those 8 dimensions, using a balanced 50:50 dataset. It reaches ROC-AUC of 0.929, failure recall of 89.1\% and F1 of 0.887 on the test set.

\subsection{HDBSCAN Clustering}

The clustering model also works on the same 8-dimensional bottleneck from the autoencoder. HDBSCAN~\cite{campello2013hdbscan,mcinnes2017hdbscan} is applied directly in that 8-dimensional space; the number of clusters is determined automatically from the data density. In our training set, 18 clusters and one outlier group emerged. Each cluster is assigned a risk score equal to its empirical failure rate in the training data. When evaluating a new disk, it is assigned to the nearest cluster and that cluster's failure rate becomes $C$ in Equation~\ref{eq:ahi}.

\subsection{Aggregated Health Index}

AHI is simply a weighted root-mean-square (RMS) formula that combines all 4 model scores into one final number representing the current risk state of the disk:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AHI} = \sqrt{\frac{w_K \cdot K^2 + w_R \cdot R^2 + w_A \cdot A^2 + w_C \cdot C^2}{\sum w}} \times 100
\label{eq:ahi}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $K$ is the random forest failure probability ($w_K = 0.30$), $R$ is the bottleneck classifier failure probability ($w_R = 0.40$), $A$ is the autoencoder anomaly score normalized to $[0,1]$ ($w_A = 0.20$), and $C$ is the empirical failure rate of the assigned cluster ($w_C = 0.10$). All scores are in $[0,1]$, weights sum to 1, and the result is multiplied by 100 to express AHI as a percentage. The final value is clipped to $[3,\,97]$ to avoid extreme boundary values. Below 40\% means \emph{Healthy}, 40--75\% is \emph{Warning}, and above 75\% is \emph{Critical}.

We evaluated AHI on 100 balanced records (50 healthy, 50 failed) from the Backblaze 2023 dataset~\cite{backblaze2023}, which was not used in training at all. Figure~\ref{fig:ahi_rock} shows how the AHI scores look for both groups.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/ahi_color_rock_holdout2023.png}
  \caption{AHI score distribution for 100 holdout disks (50 healthy, 50 failed) from the Backblaze 2023 dataset. Each dot represents one disk; vertical position is AHI (\%), color encodes AHI level from low (green) to high (red).}
  \Description{Scatter plot showing AHI percentage scores for 100 holdout disks split into two groups: 50 healthy and 50 failed. We can see that AHI correctly identified most failure records -- mean AHI for healthy disks is $33.7\%$ ($\sigma = 8.4\%$) and for failed disks it is $58.7\%$ ($\sigma = 18.0\%$), which is about a 25 percentage point difference. On the other hand, there are some visible problems in the failure group -- the standard deviation is much larger ($\sigma = 18.0\%$ vs.\ $\sigma = 8.4\%$), meaning AHI is less consistent for failing disks. This is pointing to some false negatives -- disks that are failing but have unusual SMART patterns, so they get a lower AHI score, which can be the main problem area for future improvement.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Looking at the results, we can see that no single model is the best at everything. The bottleneck classifier has the best individual numbers, while the autoencoder catches a different kind of failure -- one it can find even without seeing any failure examples during training. The clustering model adds something different on top of that: each cluster has a human-readable description of its dominant SMART pattern, so we can see not just the AHI score, but also what type of disk behavior is causing it.

The 2023 evaluation is completely out-of-sample -- no 2023 records were used at any stage of training -- so the $\sim$25 percentage point separation we see is a realistic number, not an in-sample optimistic one. The higher variance in the failure group is a known limitation. Some disks fail without showing obvious SMART problems (so-called silent failures), and those will naturally get a lower AHI regardless of the models used. To improve this, we would need either more sensor data beyond standard SMART attributes, or periodic adjustment of the weights as the fleet changes over time.

Two further limitations are worth noting. First, all training data comes from a single storage provider (Backblaze), so results may not generalize directly to other hardware vendors or real-life scenarios. Second, the system outputs a current health state rather than an estimated time to failure -- predicting a remaining useful life horizon is left for future work.

The whole pipeline is fully offline and works on a single disk snapshot, so it can run on edge devices or NAS systems without any network connection.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

In this paper, we built and evaluated a hybrid system that combines 4 independent models into a single Aggregated Health Index (AHI): a random forest classifier, an autoencoder anomaly detector, a bottleneck classifier, and an HDBSCAN clustering model. Each model captures a different kind of failure signal, and combining them with a weighted RMS formula makes the result more robust than any single model on its own. On 100 holdout records from the Backblaze 2023 dataset, mean AHI was $33.7\%$ ($\sigma = 8.4\%$) for healthy disks and $58.7\%$ ($\sigma = 18.0\%$) for failed disks -- a separation of about 25 percentage points.

For future work, it would be good to tune the weights based on data instead of setting them manually, do a full SMART feature analysis per cluster to improve the cluster descriptions, and extend the system to NVMe drives, which have a different set of SMART attributes than traditional HDDs.
